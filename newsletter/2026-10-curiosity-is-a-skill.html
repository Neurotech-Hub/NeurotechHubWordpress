Curiosity is a Skill

The Neurotech Hub Monthly  ·  October 2026

We have an event coming up where I’ll be interviewing ‘fearless innovators’. I expect you might share my love for introspecting and documenting—whether it’s a lab notebook, photography, or journalism. Tracing history often leads to the best lessons.

Discovering the thread of a story requires practice. Curiosity is a skill. Below are three quick insights I’ve found helpful in my own preparation for moving an audience.
1 Pixar helped popularize the story spine, a simple narrative structure: Once upon a time… Every day… But one day… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then… Think Toy Story, Up, and Finding Nemo.
2 Animators Trey Parker and Matt Stone actually reject stories built on “and then.” Instead, they argue each beat should connect through a “but” or “therefore.” This tactic pulls out contrast and causality in a story.
3 Terry Gross of Fresh Air is worth studying for a different reason: the way she listens. She treats each answer as a doorway, not a transaction. What’s in this room? What’s in the closet? That’s how her curiosity is felt.

iOS prototype

Kingdom App for iOS

I often look at a new tool or behavioral enclosure in here, wishing the animal it was meant for was in front of me. Kingdom for iOS is an early prototype toward that goal: using augmented reality and anatomically accurate 3D renders, Kingdom places a species of choice right in front of you.

Kingdom Tags & App. AprilTag identifiers (left) correspond to one of many species loaded into the Kingdom database. The app identifies the tag and overlays your species of choice on whatever is in the camera’s view (right).

At the Hub, we’ve scanned mice, electric fish, and even great white sharks that can all go into the app.

I envision Kingdom being used for developing new behavior tools, planning surgery, or any application that would benefit from a right-sized animal.

Interested in including your species? We would love to hear how to expand this tool to help you. Get in touch.
 

New product

ACORN: A ‘Wild’ Acoustic Tool

Intervening in an animal’s world is a key method by which scientists can better understand evolutionary, environmental, or genetic pressures that shape behavior. Sound is one such intervention that can induce drastic behavior change in many species.

We were recently asked if we could develop an auditory stimulus box that runs autonomously, can be configured rapidly, and withstands the extremes of the Canadian Yukon: yes. And at an introductory price of $799, it’s ready to scale.

Autonomous Configurable Outdoor Replay Node (ACORN). A speaker box that runs by itself with Wi-Fi-based configuration: fit for the wild or an animal facility.

ACORN can replay any audio file at a prescribed time or via external input—we’ve made this simple with our own audio converter tool. The device logs temperature, humidity, and light levels onto an SD card. Whether it’s assessing how predatory calls affect group dynamics or playing white noise to enhance slow-wave sleep, we think you’ll enjoy its large feature set and customizability.

You can connect to ACORN from any device with a web browser. Configure schedules, test playbacks, or check in on the environmental data logs.

Open source

New “StepScreen” Supports Michigan Collab

Our first Tensioned Rodent Unloading Support System (TRUSS) was deployed to the University of Michigan this month. To support its operation, we implemented a new core resource: the open source StepScreen.

Closed-loop Stepper Motor TRUSS Design. The StepScreen module makes controlling any stepper motor easy with expandability and a simple user interface in mind.

We continued to come across projects using large stepper motors, but it always felt like the control solution was hacked together. StepScreen is our one-stop solution: a brilliant LCD screen, SD card, extra GPIO, and a high-power, silent stepper motor driver all powered by a single 12V adapter.

The StepScreen Module. We’ve integrated the adaptable module (left) into many projects, including our open-top rodent treadmills to support simultaneous neural recordings during movement.
